Problem → idea

Failures are the missing data in robot learning

Task: “Stack the rings in size order.”

VLA policies learn mostly from successful demos. One small slip puts the scene outside that data, and retrying often leaves the problem in place — the robotics version of exposure bias. Recovery needs its own data, collected in failure states.

2 / 6

Robot foundation policies — VLAs — are trained mostly on successful demonstrations. At deployment, one small slip, like this ring catching on the tip of the peg, puts the scene in a state that data rarely covers. Retrying the task policy often doesn't help, because placing a ring and freeing a stuck ring are different behaviors.

If you think in sequence-model terms, it's exposure bias: trained on expert prefixes, then run on its own mistakes.

So we treat recovery as its own skill with its own data. Recova has three stages. Real-to-sim: an agent rebuilds the workstation as a digital twin and explores failures there. Sim-to-real: those rollouts train a task policy and a separate recovery policy. Real-to-data: an autonomous DAgger loop on four robots, where a VLM monitor handles failures and humans step in only at handoffs.

The headline numbers are along the bottom. I'll come back to each.

If asked

  • “0%” is one task (mahjong draw), round 4: 0 of 7 episodes. Small rounds of 7–8 episodes. Not a general zero-intervention claim.
  • Simulation numbers use a MolmoAct2 task policy plus programmatic recovery. Hardware uses π0.5-based task and recovery policies. They are different executors.
  • Analogy for DAgger: on-policy data collection. The robot visits its own failure states, and humans label only where needed.

Why two policies? Recovery is conditioned on the recovery instruction z, not the task g. Each policy trains only on data for its own role, and a recovery skill is not tied to one task.

3 / 6

First, real-to-sim-to-real. Exploring failures on hardware means constant resets and risk to objects. So a coding agent builds a simulator of the real workstation.

It takes camera images, calibration, and recorded robot trajectories. In MuJoCo, it fits object poses and contact parameters until replaying the real trajectory matches the real camera. On the left is the same recorded moment in both.

Then the agent attempts the task in the twin. When an attempt fails, it diagnoses why, names a recovery instruction, and writes a program to fix it. In the middle is the failure from the last slide — a ring stuck on the peg tip — reproduced and fixed in the twin. Across our four tasks, the agent developed 25 recovery skills this way.

Successful task rollouts train the task policy. Recovery rollouts, labeled with their instruction, train a separate recovery policy. Both start from π-zero-point-five. On the right, the learned recovery policy handles that same failure on the real robot.

We keep two policies so each one learns only its own job. And because recovery is conditioned on the instruction rather than the task, a skill isn't tied to one task.

Second, the part that scales: autonomous DAgger. One operator supervises four robot stations, each with a different background and distractors. Within a round the policies are fixed. A VLM monitor — Gemini Flash looking at all four cameras — checks completion, decides when to intervene, and names a recovery instruction.

The table is the key idea. Every episode outcome is routed to the policy it can teach. Task successes and human task demos go to the task data. Verified autonomous recoveries and human recovery demos go to the recovery data. Failed segments are logged but never trained on. The VLM works as a verifier that gates what becomes training data — similar to filtering self-generated data in LLM training.

Bottom right: four rounds on drawing a mahjong tile. The share of episodes the task policy completes rises from 12.5 to 85.7 percent. Human takeover falls from 87.5 percent to zero. In a separate 14-minute session on all four stations, the system collected 31 episodes. The task policy finished 22 of them, and human control was 11 percent of station time.

If asked

  • Rounds 1–4 takeovers: 7/8, 2/7, 2/8, 0/7. Task-policy completions: 1/8, 4/7, 6/8, 6/7. Small samples, one task.
  • Monitor: four VLM queries (requirement, completion, intervention, restoration), temperature 0, JSON replies, 448×336 four-camera composite. Median latency 4.5 s (intervention), 5.0 s (completion), 5.5 s (restoration).
  • Budgets: completion check every 600 steps (20 s), intervention every 300 steps (10 s, robot keeps acting), recovery attempt ≤ 1,500 steps (50 s), episode ≤ 5,400 steps (3 min). After two consecutive interventions, the next attempt is a full human demo.
  • New failure → the VLM proposes a new instruction z → a human demonstrates it → it also seeds a program in the twin → after training, it is registered for autonomous use.
  • The 14-min session and the four-round study are separate measurements with different denominators.
  • The video is a different recording (12:30, replayed at 10×) from the 14-min session. Station views share its clock.
  • What limits scale today: human handoffs (11% of station time in that session), manual registration of new skills, per-task policies, about 5 s per monitor check, and 1–3 h of fine-tuning per stage on 8 L40 GPUs.

5 / 6

Now the benchmarks. In simulation we compare with agentic code-as-policy methods and with VLA policies, using their published numbers. LIBERO-Pro perturbs object positions and instructions. MolmoSpaces covers picking, pick-and-place, opening, and closing across diverse scenes.

Recova reaches 78.8 percent mean success on LIBERO-Pro. That's 7.1 points above ASPIRE, the strongest baseline. On MolmoSpaces it reaches 64.9 percent, 26.9 points above RATs.

Large pretrained VLAs struggle under these perturbations: π-zero-point-five gets 12.8 percent on LIBERO-Pro. Our biggest LIBERO-Pro gains are on task perturbations in the Goal and Spatial suites, 22 and 23 points. Along the bottom are agent-developed recoveries — for example, retrying a grasp with a new pose, or opening an oven from another direction.

  • Where we trail: ASPIRE leads on the Object suite (98.0 and 95.0 vs. our 91.2 and 87.2). We lead all four Goal and Spatial settings.
  • MolmoSpaces per category (Pick, P&P, Open, Close): Recova 78.7 / 40.5 / 56.5 / 83.9. Pick-and-place is hardest: 40.5 vs. 32.0 for MolmoAct2-DROID.

  • Baselines are published results, not re-run by us. The sim executor (MolmoAct2 + programs) differs from hardware (π0.5 policies).

Real robots: 23.8% → 87.5% mean task success

Recovery is best on every task. It helps most where one misplaced object blocks every later step: +15 on pencil box and rings, +5 on mahjong, where DAgger already reaches 80%+.

Takeaway

Recovery is a learnable skill alongside task execution, and an agent can turn each failure into training data.

Selected recoveries by learned policies on hardware. Next: register new skills automatically and share them across tasks.

6 / 6

Finally, real robots: four bimanual tasks, twenty trials per task and configuration. The base task policy averages 23.8 percent. Fine-tuning on the DAgger data more than triples that, to 77.5. Turning on the recovery skills adds another 10 points, to 87.5 — the best result on every task.

Recovery helps most on pencil-box packing and ring stacking, 15 points each, where a single misplaced object blocks every step after it. On mahjong, where DAgger already reaches at least 80 percent, it still adds 5.

To wrap up: Recova treats recovery as a learnable skill alongside task execution. An agent turns each failure into data — first in simulation, then on real robots. Next, we want to register new skills automatically and share them across tasks. Happy to go deeper on any part.
